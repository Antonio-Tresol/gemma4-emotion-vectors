Does a language model keep track of
how a story feels?

We replicated Anthropic's emotion-vector work on Gemma 4 31B, then pushed it somewhere the paper did not go: stories where the emotion changes. The base model reproduces the emotion geometry almost exactly. The instruction-tuned model does not, and what replaced it is the most interesting thing we found.

What we were trying to find out

1Three questions, in order

1. Does it replicate?

Anthropic reports that emotion vectors, one direction per emotion, lay out in a valence-by-arousal plane: the circumplex psychologists have used for decades. We rebuilt it on a different model, on both the base and the instruction-tuned checkpoint.

2. Why do the two models differ?

They diverge sharply. Instruction tuning does not erase the emotion structure, it demotes it and puts something else in the top slot. We went looking for what.

3. Does it track a story?

A vector that only labels static text is a dictionary. We wrote stories that move through three emotions and asked whether the model follows along, token by token.

Why should anyone care whether this works?

Five reasons, and they pull in different directions. Some want the representation to be strong; one of them is interesting precisely if it turns out weak.

A lever to pull

A readable, causal representation of emotional state is something you can inspect and something you can move. That is interpretability (what is it representing?) and safety (what happens when you push on it?) in one object.

Models as listeners

People already bring models their worst days, and products are being sold as companions and therapists. That job is not sentiment classification on one message: it is following how someone's state moves across a long conversation. Precisely what we tested, and precisely where it gets shaky.

Internal evidence, for the welfare question

Arguments about model welfare mostly run on what a model says. Emotion vectors are one of the few handles on internal state instead. A caveat we take seriously: we measured the model reading emotions in a story, not having them. Those are different claims, and conflating them is the easiest mistake in this area.

How it carves up the world

The emotion space we recover is the model's, not ours. Where it disagrees with human ratings, that gap is a map of what is idiosyncratic about its concepts, and instruction tuning visibly redraws it.

Or: it may just not be very emotional

The interesting possibility we cannot rule out. Maybe emotion is simply not a load-bearing axis for this model, and we keep finding a weak signal because that is all there is. A null here would be a real finding about what these systems represent, not a failed experiment.

Finding 5

6Different layers are good at different things

There is no single "emotion layer". Naming the current emotion is best early; anticipating the next one is best late. Those are different jobs, done in different places.

names the right emotion (top-1 rate) anticipates the size of the coming jump

Two different competences

Layer 6 names the current emotion best (58% top-1). By layer 33 that has fallen to 27%. But the ability to anticipate how big the next emotional jump will be climbs the other way, from +0.03 at layer 6 to +0.26 at layer 51.

Even when it is wrong, it is wrong nearby: the emotion it picks instead is consistently closer in human valence-arousal space than a random emotion would be, at every single layer.

A late surprise At layer 33, the model's own probe geometry predicts its confusions better than human ratings do. At layer 51 that reverses. Our headline finding is layer-dependent, and we only noticed because every figure re-computes its verdict per layer.

Finding 6

7Where the vectors come from matters more than we expected

Every emotion vector is built by averaging the model's activations over stories that evoke that emotion. So who writes those stories is a free parameter, and nobody reports it. We built four probe sets from four different authors and scored them identically.

layers where the probes pass the registered detection bar (8 of 12 scenarios, on two separate batteries)
How to read this

Each bar is one probe set, differing only in who wrote the stories behind it. Height is how many of the 20 tested layers pass a bar that was registered before any of this was scored. Zero would mean the probes never work; the best we saw is 9.

The ordering is the finding. A stronger external writer (DeepSeek) gives probes that work at nine layers. The model's own writing gives five. A weaker model's writing gives one. So "the model understands its own emotions best" is false here: it is out-written.

source: notebooks/07_generator_lineages.ipynb (experiments E11 and E12)

Why its own stories are worse

Asked for 3,072 emotional stories, the model wrote a character named Elias in 98.2% of them. Its self-written corpus repeats 5-word phrases 53× more often than DeepSeek's. Narrow, repetitive text makes a narrow vector: the probe learns the model's writing habits alongside the emotion.

This is also the clearest thing we saw about what is idiosyncratic to this model. Ask it for variety and it gives you Elias, 3,000 times.

How many stories do you actually need?

About 64 per emotion. By then the probes reach 8.6 of their 9-layer ceiling, and 4× more data buys almost nothing.

Where this leaves us

8What we would do next

Name the new axis

The largest direction in the instruct model is not valence, arousal, dominance, or story length. Identify it: steer along it and see what changes in the model's behaviour.

Separate probe from model

Attractors appeared in one probe bank and not the other. Probe-set construction is doing more work than anyone reports. That deserves its own experiment.

Test the anticipation

The model appears to lean into an emotional turn before it arrives. Is that reading the setup, or predicting the next token? A story whose turn is unforeseeable would separate the two.

What we are confident in, and what we are not Confident: the base model reproduces the circumplex; the instruct model does not, and one non-affective axis dominates it. Emotion probes do track a story better than chance. Not confident: what the new axis is; how much of the tracking quality is the probe set rather than the model. Everything here is registered in a research tree with the evidence file behind each number, including the results that came out null.
The reading we cannot yet rule out, and would like to Every positive result here is real but small: above chance, below any bar you would want before trusting it. The tidy story is that emotion is represented and we are measuring it imperfectly. The other story is that emotion is simply not a major axis of this model's world, and a weak signal is the honest answer rather than a measurement problem. Distinguishing those two is the experiment we would run next, and it is the more interesting question either way.
How we measured it

9Method, in enough detail to argue with

Each block is collapsed. Open the one you want to challenge.

1. How an emotion becomes a vector

We take a corpus where every story is labelled with the emotion it is written to evoke, feed each story to the model, and record the activations at a chosen layer. For one emotion, we average those activations over all its stories. That gives one vector per emotion, 171 of them.

Two details that change the numbers. Centering: we subtract the mean over all 12 or 171 emotions, so what is left is what makes this emotion different rather than what all text has in common. Skipping this step is the difference between a result and an artefact. Which tokens: a story is many tokens, and we pool them: mask the padding, drop the first 50 as narrative framing (the source paper's convention), and average over the rest, capped at 512 tokens. Both choices were fixed before scoring. Notice this differs from the story-tracking read in block 3, where we deliberately keep every token instead of pooling.

2. What "PC1 = valence, |r| 0.83" actually means

This is the one worth spelling out, because the phrase hides four steps.

Step one. We have 171 emotion vectors, each thousands of numbers long. Principal component analysis finds the directions along which those 171 points differ most. The first component (PC1) is the single direction that captures the most spread. It is derived only from the model: no human labels are involved in finding it.

Step two. Each of the 171 emotions gets a score on PC1: how far along that direction it sits. So "elated" gets a number, "miserable" gets a number, and so on.

Step three. Separately, we look up each emotion word in the NRC VAD lexicon, a published set of human ratings where people scored thousands of words for valence, arousal and dominance. 164 of our 171 words are in it.

Step four. We correlate the model's 164 PC1 scores against the humans' 164 valence ratings. That correlation is 0.83 on the base model. In words: the direction of largest variation in the model's emotion space, found without reference to any human judgement, lines up strongly with what people call pleasant versus unpleasant.

Why |r| and not r. The sign of a principal component is arbitrary, so we report absolute correlation. What would count as failure: |r| near 0, meaning the model's biggest axis has nothing to do with valence. That is close to what the instruct model gives (0.11).

3. How "does it track the story?" was scored

We wrote 173 three-emotion story sets, each moving through three tagged phases. Here nothing is pooled away: the model reads the story once and we keep the activation at every token, then take the cosine against each of 12 emotion probes at each position. A phase's score is the mean over the tokens inside that phase; phases shorter than 4 tokens are skipped rather than averaged. Two questions follow, both with pre-registered answers.

Naming (the gate): within a phase written as "afraid", where does the afraid probe rank among the 12? Rank 1 is perfect, 6.5 is guessing. Anticipation (the lead): in the window before a written turn, does the next emotion's probe already start to lead? Zero would mean the model does not see the turn coming.

Both are compared against a wrong-emotion shuffle: the same computation with the labels permuted. That shuffle, not our intuition, is the floor.

4. What kept us honest

Every prediction and its pass bar was written into a research tree before the data existed. Claims only graduate after a falsification pass: permutation nulls, bootstrap confidence intervals clustered by story, random-direction controls, and base-rate checks. Results that failed are recorded as failed. Every number in this deck resolves to a file in the repository, and the notebooks re-run from those files on any machine.

One convention change is worth flagging because it flipped a headline: our first detection campaign found nothing passing, and a later audit showed the readout had skipped the centering step in step 1. With centering, the same data passes on both models. We report both.

Appendix

10Questions we expect, and our honest answers

Collapsed, so we can jump to whichever one gets asked.

Isn't this just sentiment analysis with extra steps?

Sentiment analysis gives one label for a span of text. We are reading a direction in the model's internal state, at every token, without asking the model anything. That lets us do two things a classifier cannot: watch the representation move through a story, and intervene on it. The interesting result is not the label, it is that valence organises the space on its own.

Does the model actually feel anything?

Nothing here shows that, and we would resist the leap. We measured the model representing emotions in a story it is reading, which is closer to reading comprehension than to having a feeling. It is relevant as internal evidence, because most welfare arguments run on what a model says rather than on its internals, but reading an emotion and having one are different claims.

The correlations are small. Isn't this noise?

Some of them are, and we say so. The geometry result is not small: |r| 0.83 on 164 words is a strong effect. The tracking results are modest and beat a shuffle null at p below 0.001. The intensity result we deliberately report as sign-only, because a control showed random directions reach the same correlation size, so only the direction is evidence.

Why Gemma 4 and not the model in the paper?

We could run both a base and an instruction-tuned checkpoint of the same size, which is exactly the comparison the paper cannot make. That constraint produced our main finding.

Could the instruct PC1 just be an artefact of your pipeline?

We checked the obvious ones. It is not story length (|r| 0.39, and PC2 tracks length harder at 0.66). It survives an extraction-convention audit that changed other numbers. It is not a sign flip or a rotation, since its best match against any base component is 0.14. What it is remains open, and we would rather say that than name it.

How much of the tracking result is your probe set rather than the model?

More than we would like, and that is a finding in itself. Swapping who wrote the stories behind the probes moves passing layers from 1 to 9, and the confusion attractors present in one bank vanish in another. Any paper reporting emotion-probe accuracy without reporting probe provenance is under-specified.

Why does layer 6 name emotions better than layer 33?

We do not know, and it is our favourite loose end. One hypothesis: early layers stay closer to surface lexical cues, and our stories contain emotional words, so a shallow reader does well. The deciding test is a corpus where the emotion is implied but never lexically present.

What would have changed your mind?

For the geometry: a base model whose PC1 did not correlate with valence. For the tracking: probe ranks indistinguishable from the wrong-emotion shuffle, which is what the constant-emotion control arm came close to. We ran that control precisely so a null had somewhere to show up.

What is the single weakest part of this work?

The absolute effect sizes on tracking. Everything is above chance and below where you would want it before trusting it in a product. We cannot yet separate 'weak measurement of a real thing' from 'accurate measurement of a weak thing'.

If you remember one thing The base model reproduces the emotion circumplex almost exactly. Instruction tuning does not destroy that structure, it demotes it and puts one large non-affective axis on top. And whichever model you ask, how well its emotion probes work depends heavily on who wrote the stories you built them from.
