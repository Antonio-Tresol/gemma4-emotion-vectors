The question is then whether those model-found axes mean anything to us. So we take each axis and correlate it against published human ratings of the same 171 words. Those ratings cover three things: how pleasant the emotion is (psychologists call this valence), how worked-up (arousal), and how in control the person feels (dominance). We also check it against one thing that has nothing to do with emotion but could fake the whole result: how long the stories were. A perfect match scores 1.0; no relationship scores 0.

How to read this

What a success looks like: the base model. Its biggest axis matches human valence ratings at 0.83, and its second matches energy at 0.55. Those are the two axes of the circumplex, in order, found by a method that never saw a human rating. That is the published result, reproduced.

One honest complication. That biggest axis also matches a third human rating, dominance (how much control the emotion implies), at 0.66, higher than the second axis matches energy. Valence and dominance are themselves correlated in the human ratings, so this figure alone cannot say whether the axis is valence, or valence and dominance together. Story length is ruled out: it matches this axis at 0.15. We have not separated these two, and the result we are reproducing carries the same ambiguity.

What a failure looks like: the instruction-tuned model. Its biggest axis matches valence at 0.11, near zero. The 171 emotions differ from one another in many ways at once. This single axis accounts for 28% of all that variation, against 15% for the base model's top axis. So it is not merely a big axis, it is nearly twice as dominant, and it is not valence.

Valence has not vanished, it has moved down: the instruction-tuned model's third axis matches it at 0.72. And watch the amber bar. The instruction-tuned model's second axis matches story length at 0.66, harder than it matches any emotion rating. A big axis is not automatically an emotional one.
