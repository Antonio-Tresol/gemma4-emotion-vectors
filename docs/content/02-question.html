1What we measured, and why it matters

Building an emotion vector

We built each emotion vector by averaging the model's internal state across one emotion's stories, then subtracting the average over all the emotions. To read it back we measure the angle between that vector and the model's state at a given word, which is what a cosine is here. The cosine is centred: we subtract the mean over the story set first, so 0 is the corpus average. Section 10 gives the formula, the pseudo-code and a glossary for every symbol in it.

Parts I and II use 171 emotions with about 9 stories each. Part III uses 12 emotions with about 256 stories each. Section 2 measures how much that count matters.

We compare Gemma 4 31B in two forms, base and instruction-tuned: the same weights before and after post-training. We measured at twenty of the model's sixty layers, every third from layer 0 to layer 57, for the principal-component and detection experiments in sections 2 and 3. The story experiments in sections 6 to 8 use six of those layers.

The result being replicated is that emotion vectors arrange on the circumplex. Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the directions along which they differ most, and the top two recover valence and arousal. That matches how humans rate the same words in the NRC VAD lexicon (VAD is valence, arousal, dominance). The components are computed from the activations alone, and the human ratings are brought in afterwards to test whether the axes line up with them.

Why this matters

  • A direction can be read and added back in. The same object serves as an interpretability measurement and as an intervention, which is unusual and is what makes emotion vectors worth testing at all.
  • Models are already used as listeners. People bring them their worst days, and companies are selling them as companions and therapists. Doing that well means following how someone's state moves across a long conversation, which is what we tested and where it gets shaky. Emotion may not be a major axis of this model's representation at all, and that is the possibility we find most interesting.
  • AI welfare needs evidence that is not self-report. Arguments in that field lean heavily on what a model says about itself, and reading internal state sidesteps the obvious objection to that. The caveat is load-bearing: what we measured is the model representing the emotions of a story it is reading. That is not evidence about what the model feels.
  • The geometry departs from human ratings in measurable ways. Where the model's arrangement of emotions diverges from the VAD norms, the difference is itself a measurement of what is peculiar to this model, and instruction tuning visibly redraws it.
Part I

Rebuilding the published result

Sections 2 and 3 rebuild the paper's central result on an open model. Sorted by what separates them most, a model's emotion vectors should recover the pleasantness and activation axes psychologists use. Section 2 comes first because the rebuild depends on which stories the vectors are built from, and that turns out to change the answer by a factor of nine.
