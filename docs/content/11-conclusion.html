10Conclusion

We have shown that an open 31-billion-parameter model organises emotions much the way psychologists do. The biggest axis of the base model's 171 emotion vectors is pleasant versus unpleasant, matching human valence ratings at 0.83, with no human labels in the sorting. Instruction tuning keeps that axis but demotes it to third place, beneath a larger axis we could not identify. And the vectors are not inert. Read out token by token, they follow a story's emotional turns better than chance, usually leaning towards the next emotion before the written turn. Added back into the model, ten of the twelve move its preferences the way their valence predicts.

We caution against reading more into these directions than the measurements support. Every positive result on this page is better than chance and too weak to rely on. Part of that weakness sits in our instruments rather than the model: section 7's pile-up of wrong answers onto two emotions appeared with one writer's vectors and vanished with another's. How much of the rest is the model and how much the instruments is an experiment section 9 already lists. And nothing here speaks to whether the model feels anything; we measured directions and their effects, not experience.

Even so, the directions are there, they are readable, and they move behaviour. Instruction tuning, the step that turns a base model into an assistant, is also the step that rearranged them. Understanding what it put on top is where we would start.
