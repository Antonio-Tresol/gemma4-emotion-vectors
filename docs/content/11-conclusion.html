10Conclusion

We observed that an open 31-billion-parameter model organises emotions much the way psychologists do. The biggest axis of the base model's 171 emotion vectors is pleasant versus unpleasant, matching human valence ratings at 0.83, with no human labels in the sorting. Instruction tuning keeps that axis but demotes it to third place, beneath a larger axis we could not identify. And the vectors are not inert. Read out token by token, they follow a story's emotional turns better than chance, usually leaning towards the next emotion before the written turn. Added back into the model, ten of the twelve move its preferences the way their valence predicts.

We would urge caution in drawing strong conclusions from these directions. Every positive result on this page is real but small: each clears its chance level and none approaches the ceiling. Some of that gap belongs to the vector sets rather than the model: section 7's concentration of errors onto two emotions appeared with one writer's vectors and vanished with another's. Separating the rest is the third experiment in section 9. And our measurements concern emotion representations and their effects on behaviour, not whether the model feels anything.

Even so, the directions are there, they are readable, and they move behaviour. Instruction tuning, the step that turns a base model into an assistant, is also the step that rearranged them. Understanding what it put on top is where we would start.
