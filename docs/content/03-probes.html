2Who writes the stories changes the answer

An emotion vector is only as good as the corpus behind it, and nothing in the method says what that corpus should be. We built four sets from four different corpora and put them through the same test.

The test. Take a short message of the kind a person actually sends a chatbot, say "My daughter just took her first steps today! What are some ways to capture more of these precious moments?". It implies an emotion without ever naming it. Run it as a chat turn, and read the residual stream at the last token before the reply would start. Rank all twelve emotion vectors by cosine and ask whether the right one lands in the top three.

Two things make this harder than it sounds. The vectors were built from stories and are being tested on messages, so nothing guarantees transfer. And the emotion is never stated, so matching on the words cannot work. A layer counts as working when it gets at least 8 of 12 right on the paper's twelve scenarios and on a second twelve we wrote ourselves before scoring anything. Both marks were fixed in advance.

Darker is more scenarios correct. A row's number is its ringed cells: the layers where these vectors detect the emotion behind a message. 0 of 20 would mean they never work at all.
How to read this

One row per story source, one column per sampled layer. The cell is how many of the twelve scenarios that layer got right, taking the worse of the two sets of twelve. A layer has to clear the mark on both. Ringed cells clear it. The number on the right is how many rings the row has.

The grading scale. 0 of 20 layers would mean these vectors never detect anything. 20 of 20 would mean every depth works. Nothing we built came close to the top: the best story source reaches 9, and the worst reaches 1.

The ordering is the finding. A stronger outside writer (DeepSeek) gives vectors that work at nine layers; the model's own writing gives five; a smaller model's writing gives one. So the intuitive guess, that a model reads its own emotional writing best, is wrong here. It is out-written.

The four corpora differ in more than their writer. Two of them are DeepSeek's, written under different prompts. Their sizes run from 1,539 stories to 12,262, as the row labels show. So these rows compare whole corpora. They do not isolate the writer.

Why two DeepSeek rows? One used a single fixed prompt. The other used a persona-by-setting grid meant to force variety. The varied corpus is four times the size and still scores lower, which is the opposite of what we expected. A separate run controls for size properly, with 5 seeds per point and the same scorer. Matched on stories per emotion, the fixed-prompt corpus reaches 8.6 passing layers at 64 stories each. The varied one needs 1,024 each to reach 6.8. Forcing variety costs accuracy here rather than buying it.

Where matters as well as how many. No source works in the first third of the stack, and the passing layers cluster late, around 33 to 57. A single quoted layer would have hidden that.

Why its own stories are worse

Asked for 3,072 stories about emotions, the model named its character Elias in 98.2% of them. Its own writing reuses five-word phrases 53× as often as DeepSeek's does. That matters because narrow, repetitive stories make a narrow vector. Whatever the stories have in common gets averaged in, so the vector stands for the model's writing habits as much as for the emotion.

It is also the sharpest thing we saw about what is peculiar to this particular model. Ask it for variety and it gives you Elias, 3,000 times.

How many stories do you actually need?

About 64 per emotion. At that point the vectors already reach 8.6 working layers out of the 9 this writer ever achieves. (It is a fraction because it averages five random draws of that many stories.) Quadrupling the stories buys almost nothing.

TakeawayAn emotion vector is only as good as the corpus behind it. Same model, same test, and a nine-fold spread in how many layers work. A stronger outside writer beats the model's own stories, which are too repetitive to build a broad vector from.
